Why rivers never run straight

Give water a slope and a bank to push against, and it starts to swing. A short guide to meanders, from the first bend to the oxbow lake.

By Ines Ward · 8 min read

Look down on almost any lowland river and you will see the same thing: loops. Straight stretches rarely run for more than about ten times the channel's width before the water starts to swing from one bank to the other. It isn't chance. Once a bend begins, the river does almost everything it can to make it bigger.

It starts with the fastest water. In a straight channel the current runs quickest down the middle, but the smallest disturbance, a fallen tree, a shingle bar or a patch of softer clay, nudges that thread of fast water towards one bank. Where it hits, it erodes. Where it leaves, the slower water drops the sand and gravel it was carrying.

Cut banks and point bars

On the outside of a bend the river undercuts its bank, leaving a steep wall called a cut bank. On the inside it builds a gentle slope of sediment called a point bar. Erosion on one side and deposition on the other mean the whole bend slowly migrates outwards and downstream, sometimes by metres a year.

Below the surface the water is doing something stranger. As it rounds the bend it piles up slightly against the outer bank, and that extra height drives a slow corkscrew of flow: surface water heads for the outside, sinks, and returns along the bed towards the inside. This helical flow is what carries sediment across the channel to build the point bar.

Erosion on one side and deposition on the other mean the whole bend slowly walks across its valley.

From loop to lake

Left alone, neighbouring loops creep towards each other until only a narrow neck of land separates them. A flood is often enough to break through. The river takes the short cut, and the abandoned loop, sealed off by fresh deposits, becomes a crescent-shaped oxbow lake.

Geographers measure all this with sinuosity: the length of the channel divided by the straight-line distance down the valley. A ratio of 1.5 is enough to call a river meandering, and the most tortuous lowland rivers loop so much that they travel two or three times as far as the crow flies.

Why it matters

Meanders slow floods down, sort gravel for spawning fish and build the flat, fertile floodplains that farms and towns have always been drawn to. Straightening rivers to drain land or speed up shipping often backfires: the faster water scours its bed, floods arrive downstream sooner, and engineers spend decades putting the bends back.

So the next time a river seems to be taking the long way round, give it some credit. Every loop is the result of a long argument between water and ground, and the water is very patient.
